## 1.1 Where this fits

Before an AIS message reaches your program it is wrapped in an NMEA 0183
sentence (chapter 0, layer 3). NMEA 0183 is a text protocol from 1983 in
which one *talker* (a GPS receiver, a gyro, an AIS transponder) sends short
ASCII lines to any number of *listeners* over a serial link
[NMEA 0183], [IEC 61162-1 §7]. The international version of the standard
is IEC 61162-1; the two documents describe the same sentences and we cite
whichever is more specific.

This chapter teaches the sentence format on the gentlest possible data: a
small Bluetooth GPS receiver that emits nothing but four standard sentences
once a second. There is no six-bit payload to unpack and every field is
readable text, so you can concentrate on the envelope — the part that
*every* later chapter relies on. Chapter 2 then puts AIS inside the same
envelope.

### Operational use

On a ship the NMEA 0183 bus is the nervous system of the bridge: the GNSS
receiver's `GGA`/`RMC` sentences feed the chart display, the autopilot, the
radar and the AIS transponder itself, which must take its position from an
external electronic position-fixing device when one is connected
[IEC 61993-2]. The same sentences come out of handheld and automotive
receivers, which is why the format is familiar to anyone who has looked at
a GPS serial port. The four sentences in this chapter answer the four
questions a navigator asks: *where am I and how good is the fix* (`GGA`),
*which satellites are being used and how is the geometry* (`GSA`), *what is
in the sky* (`GSV`), and *where am I going, how fast, and what day is it*
(`RMC`).

### Unattended / automated uses

Machines read far more NMEA than people do. Data loggers such as the one
that recorded `sample-data/` append a station id and a timestamp to every
sentence and never look inside (§1.2.6). Time servers discipline a
computer's clock from the `RMC` date and time. Fleet trackers, photo
geotaggers and survey systems all parse `GGA` and `RMC` and trust the
result — and that is where the trouble starts. A receiver that has not yet
acquired a fix still emits `GGA` and `RMC` sentences with a *status* field
saying so, and the position fields may contain a placeholder; our own data
(§1.3, §1.6) show a receiver reporting latitude "89°60′" before lock. An
unattended consumer that does not check the fix-quality and status fields
plots that at the North Pole. Likewise, the altitude field is height above
the geoid, not above the ellipsoid, and a logger that files it as the
latter is 20 m wrong at the position in our data. These sentences are
simple; the discipline they demand of software is not.

## 1.2 The specification

### 1.2.1 Sentence anatomy

Every sentence has the same skeleton [IEC 61162-1 §7], [NMEA 0183],
[AIVDM "AIVDM/AIVDO Sentence Layer"]:

```text
$GPGGA,000429.000,3601.5522,N,10947.9359,W,1,7,4.21,12491.0,M,-20.5,M,,*6D<CR><LF>
│└┤└┤ └──────────────────────── data fields ────────────────────────┘│└┤
│ │  └ formatter (3 characters)                                      │ └ checksum: two hex digits
│ └ talker (2 characters)                                            └ delimiter
└ start: '$' = parametric sentence, '!' = encapsulation sentence
```

| Part | Rule | Citation |
|---|---|---|
| Start delimiter | `$` for an ordinary ("parametric") sentence, `!` for an *encapsulation* sentence whose data field carries binary data in six-bit form — AIS `VDM`/`VDO`, chapter 2 | [IEC 61162-1 §7]; [AIVDM "AIVDM/AIVDO Sentence Layer"] |
| Address field | Immediately after the delimiter, no comma: a two-character **talker** identifier and a three-character **sentence formatter** (`GP` + `GGA`). Proprietary sentences use `P` + a three-character manufacturer code instead (§1.2.2) | [IEC 61162-1 §7]; [NMEA 0183] |
| Data fields | Separated by commas; the number and meaning are fixed per formatter (§1.2.5). A field may be *empty* (two commas in a row), meaning "no data" (§1.2.4) | [IEC 61162-1 §7] |
| Checksum | `*` followed by two upper-case hexadecimal digits: the bitwise exclusive-OR of every character between the start delimiter and the `*`, both excluded (§1.2.3) | [IEC 61162-1 §7.2.3]; [AIVDM "AIVDM/AIVDO Sentence Layer"] |
| Terminator | Carriage return, line feed (`\r\n`, hex `0D 0A`) | [IEC 61162-1 §7] |
| Length | At most 82 characters from `$` to `<LF>` inclusive — 80 printable characters including the `*hh` — which is the reason long AIS messages are split over several sentences | [IEC 61162-1 §7]; [AIVDM "AIVDM/AIVDO Sentence Layer"] |
| Character set | Printable ASCII; the reserved characters `$ ! * , \ ^ ~` and `<CR><LF>` may not occur inside a field | [IEC 61162-1 §7] |

The `GGA` above is 76 characters including CR LF. Receivers do not always
respect the limit — shore networks re-join AIS fragments into 140-character
lines (chapter 2) — so our parsers do not enforce it.

### 1.2.2 Talkers and proprietary sentences

The talker says what *kind of equipment* wrote the sentence. The ones you
will meet in this book:

| Talker | Equipment | Citation |
|---|---|---|
| `GP` | GPS receiver (the only talker in this chapter's data) | [NMEA 0183]; [IEC 61162-1 §7] |
| `GL`, `GA`, `GB` | GLONASS, Galileo, BeiDou receivers | [NMEA 0183] |
| `GN` | Combined multi-constellation GNSS solution — what a modern receiver emits in place of `GP` | [NMEA 0183] |
| `AI` | Mobile AIS station (Class A, Class B, SAR aircraft) — also used by almost every AIS receiver | [AIVDM "Talker IDs"]; [IEC 61162-1 §7] |
| `AB` | AIS base station (NMEA 4.0 and later) | [AIVDM "Talker IDs"] |
| `BS` | AIS base station, older form, deprecated in NMEA 4.0 | [AIVDM "Talker IDs"] |
| `AN` | AIS aid-to-navigation station | [AIVDM "Talker IDs"] |
| `AR` | AIS receiving station (a receive-only shore unit) | [AIVDM "Talker IDs"] |
| `SA` | Physical shore AIS station | [AIVDM "Talker IDs"] |
| `AD`, `AS`, `AT`, `AX` | Dependent base station, limited base station, transmitting station, repeater (rare) | [AIVDM "Talker IDs"] |
| `P` + 3 letters | **Proprietary**: `P` followed by a manufacturer mnemonic (`PMTK` = MediaTek, `PSRF` = SiRF, `PGRM` = Garmin, `PFEC` = Furuno); the rest of the sentence is defined by the manufacturer, not by the standard | [IEC 61162-1 §7]; [NMEA 0183] |

Our parser therefore splits the address field in two ways: `GPGGA` →
talker `GP`, formatter `GGA`; but `PMTK010` → talker `P`, formatter
`MTK010`. Two proprietary sentences occur in the data:

* `$PMTK010,001*2E` — a MediaTek "system message" that the chipset sends
  once at power-up (`001` = startup, per MediaTek's *MTK NMEA Packet User
  Manual*, which is not among this book's references). It has a correct
  checksum; our driver reports it as `"skipped":"no parser"`.
* `$POLYN,TIME,RESTART_OCCURRED`, `$POLYN,EPH,7,0D441080`,
  `$POLYN,ALM,31,EFFFFFFF`, `$POLYN,ION`, `$POLYN,UTC` — five lines the same
  chipset prints during a cold start that look like a report of what it
  still remembers (ephemeris for 7 satellites, almanac for 31, ionosphere
  and UTC parameters). They carry **no checksum at all**, so they are not
  valid NMEA sentences and our driver reports `"error":"no checksum"`
  (§1.5). We have found no documentation for them; treat the
  interpretation as a guess.

### 1.2.3 The checksum

The checksum is the exclusive-OR (XOR) of the ASCII codes of every
character *between* the start delimiter and the `*`, written as two
upper-case hexadecimal digits [IEC 61162-1 §7.2.3],
[AIVDM "AIVDM/AIVDO Sentence Layer"]. XOR is the bit-by-bit operation
"1 if the bits differ, 0 if they are equal". Because XOR of a value with
itself is 0, the checksum detects any single corrupted character and most
multi-character corruptions, but it cannot detect two characters swapped
or the same bit flipped twice.

Take the shortest sentence in our data, line 9 of
`data/ch01-gps-itrek-coldstart.nmea`: `$GPGSV,1,1,00*79` — a receiver
reporting zero satellites in view. The body is `GPGSV,1,1,00` (twelve
characters). Start with 0 and XOR each character's ASCII code in turn:

```text
char  ASCII  binary     running XOR
 G     71    01000111   01000111  = 0x47
 P     80    01010000   00010111  = 0x17
 G     71    01000111   01010000  = 0x50
 S     83    01010011   00000011  = 0x03
 V     86    01010110   01010101  = 0x55
 ,     44    00101100   01111001  = 0x79
 1     49    00110001   01001000  = 0x48
 ,     44    00101100   01100100  = 0x64
 1     49    00110001   01010101  = 0x55
 ,     44    00101100   01111001  = 0x79
 0     48    00110000   01001001  = 0x49
 0     48    00110000   01111001  = 0x79
```

`0x79` is what the sentence says, so the checksum is good. Notice two
things: the two `1`s cancel each other, the second and third `,` cancel
each other and the two `0`s cancel each other — XOR is its own inverse —
so the result after `GPGSV,` is already `0x79`, which is why
`$GPGSV,1,1,00` and `$GPGSV,` *alone* would have the same checksum. That is the weakness of XOR as an integrity check;
it is good enough for a serial cable and no more.

Two practical rules follow. The checksum covers *only* the text up to
`*`; anything after the two hex digits — in our logs, the
`,rplane_to_sd,1197072268.78` suffix — is not included and must not be
fed to the XOR (§1.2.6). And the sentence is checked, not trusted: a
sentence with a bad checksum is discarded, not repaired.

### 1.2.4 Fields, empty fields and the position format

Data fields are numbered from 1 starting *after* the address field, which
is how the standard's field tables count them and how the code's
docstrings are written; in the code, `fields[0]` is field 1. A sentence
formatter fixes the number of fields, but later editions of the standard
have *appended* fields to several formatters (`RMC` grew a mode indicator
in NMEA 2.3 and a navigational-status field in 4.10 [NMEA 0183]), so a
robust parser pads a short field list with empty strings rather than
indexing past the end.

An **empty field** — nothing between two commas — means "data not
available" [IEC 61162-1 §7]. It is not zero: a `GGA` with an empty HDOP
field has *no* HDOP, and `GSV` lists satellites whose elevation and
azimuth are empty because the receiver has heard them but not yet
decoded where they are. Every field accessor in our code therefore
returns an optional value (`None`, `std::nullopt`, `None`, `null`) for an
empty field, and the JSON output prints `null`.

**Time** is `hhmmss.ss` UTC with any number of fractional digits (the
i.Trek writes three), and **date** in `RMC` is `ddmmyy` — six digits, two
for the year [IEC 61162-1 §8 (RMC)]. NMEA predates the year 2000 and the
standard never gave the century; we assume 2000–2099, which is right for
every file in this book (§1.6).

**Latitude and longitude** are the one format that trips up every
newcomer. They are not decimal degrees. Latitude is `ddmm.mmmm` — two
digits of whole degrees, then minutes with a decimal fraction — followed by
a separate `N`/`S` field; longitude is `dddmm.mmmm` with *three* degree
digits and `E`/`W` [IEC 61162-1 §7; §8 (GGA)]. The number of decimal
places on the minutes is up to the receiver (the i.Trek writes four,
survey receivers write more). To convert:

```text
3601.5522,N   -> 36 + 01.5522 / 60 = 36.025870°   (north positive)
10947.9359,W  -> 109 + 47.9359 / 60 = 109.798932° -> -109.798932° (west negative)
```

Four decimal places of a minute are 0.0001′ ≈ 0.19 m on the ground; six
decimal places of a degree are ≈ 0.11 m, so printing six decimals loses
nothing. That is why every latitude and longitude in this book's output is
a `Fixed(value, 6)`. The hemisphere letter is a separate field, so the
degrees part is never signed — a receiver that printed `-3601.5522` would
be broken.

### 1.2.5 The four GPS sentences

The i.Trek receiver emits a fixed cycle once a second: one `GGA`, one
`GSA`, two or three `GSV`, one `RMC`. Field numbers are as in the
standards and in the docstrings of `code/python/aistut/nmea.py`. The
"Output" column is the key in the JSON that our decoders print
(§1.5); where a value is non-integer, the number of decimals is given.

**GGA — Global positioning system fix data** [IEC 61162-1 §8 (GGA)],
[NMEA 0183], [NMEA Revealed "GGA"]:

| # | Field | Format / units | Empty means | Output |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | UTC of position | `hhmmss.ss` | no time | `time_utc` as `"hh:mm:ss.sss"` text |
| 2 | Latitude | `ddmm.mmmm` | no position | `lat`, decimal degrees, 6 decimals |
| 3 | N/S | `N` or `S` | — | (sign of `lat`) |
| 4 | Longitude | `dddmm.mmmm` | no position | `lon`, decimal degrees, 6 decimals |
| 5 | E/W | `E` or `W` | — | (sign of `lon`) |
| 6 | GPS quality indicator | 0 = fix not available, 1 = GPS SPS fix, 2 = differential GPS, 3 = PPS, 4 = RTK fixed integer, 5 = RTK float, 6 = estimated (dead reckoning), 7 = manual input, 8 = simulator | — | `fix_quality` + `fix_quality_text` |
| 7 | Satellites in use | 0–12 (more on modern receivers) | — | `satellites_used` |
| 8 | Horizontal dilution of precision | dimensionless | no HDOP | `hdop`, 2 decimals |
| 9 | Antenna altitude above/below mean sea level (geoid) | metres | no altitude | `altitude_m`, 1 decimal |
| 10 | Units of altitude | always `M` | — | (not output) |
| 11 | Geoidal separation: geoid minus WGS-84 ellipsoid at this position | metres, negative where the geoid is below the ellipsoid | no value | `geoid_separation_m`, 1 decimal |
| 12 | Units of separation | always `M` | — | (not output) |
| 13 | Age of differential corrections | seconds since the last DGPS update | not using DGPS | `dgps_age_s`, 1 decimal |
| 14 | Differential reference station id | `0000`–`1023` | not using DGPS | `dgps_station` as text |

The two height fields are the ones to read twice. Field 9 is height above
the *geoid* (roughly, mean sea level), which is what a pilot or a
mountaineer wants; GPS itself measures height above the WGS-84
*ellipsoid*, and field 11 is the difference the receiver applied. Height
above the ellipsoid = field 9 + field 11 [IEC 61162-1 §8 (GGA)]. In our
flight data that is 12 491.0 + (−20.5) = 12 470.5 m.

**GSA — GNSS DOP and active satellites** [IEC 61162-1 §8 (GSA)],
[NMEA 0183], [NMEA Revealed "GSA"]:

| # | Field | Format | Empty means | Output |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Selection mode | `M` = manual (forced 2D or 3D), `A` = automatic switching | — | `selection_mode` |
| 2 | Fix mode | 1 = fix not available, 2 = 2D, 3 = 3D | — | `fix_mode` + `fix_mode_text` |
| 3–14 | Satellite ids used in the solution | up to 12 PRN numbers; unused slots empty | slot unused | `satellites_used`, a list of integers (empties dropped) |
| 15 | PDOP (position) | dimensionless | no fix | `pdop`, 2 decimals |
| 16 | HDOP (horizontal) | dimensionless | no fix | `hdop`, 2 decimals |
| 17 | VDOP (vertical) | dimensionless | no fix | `vdop`, 2 decimals |

Dilution of precision multiplies the receiver's ranging error into a
position error; below 2 is excellent, above 6 is poor. NMEA 4.11 appended
a field 18, the GNSS system id, which the padding logic simply ignores
[NMEA 0183].

**GSV — GNSS satellites in view** [IEC 61162-1 §8 (GSV)], [NMEA 0183],
[NMEA Revealed "GSV"].
One sentence holds at most four satellites, so a receiver tracking ten
sends three sentences, numbered 1–3 of 3, and **the last one is shorter**:

| # | Field | Format | Empty means | Output |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Total number of GSV sentences in this group | 1–9 | — | `total_sentences` |
| 2 | This sentence's number | 1–total | — | `sentence_number` |
| 3 | Total satellites in view | 0–… | — | `satellites_in_view` |
| 4, 8, 12, 16 | Satellite id | GPS PRN 1–32; 33–64 are SBAS satellites (PRN 120–151 minus 87), so `48` is WAAS PRN 135 [NMEA Revealed "Satellite IDs"] | — | `satellites[].prn` |
| 5, 9, 13, 17 | Elevation | degrees, 0–90 | unknown (almanac not decoded) | `satellites[].elevation_deg` |
| 6, 10, 14, 18 | Azimuth | degrees true, 0–359 | unknown | `satellites[].azimuth_deg` |
| 7, 11, 15, 19 | Signal-to-noise ratio | dB-Hz, 0–99 | not tracked | `satellites[].snr_db` |

A `GSV` group is the first multi-sentence construct in the book. Our
parser handles each sentence on its own and leaves grouping to the reader
(exercise 5); chapter 2's `Assembler` does the real thing for AIS.

**RMC — Recommended minimum specific GNSS data** [IEC 61162-1 §8 (RMC)],
[NMEA 0183], [NMEA Revealed "RMC"]:

| # | Field | Format / units | Empty means | Output |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | UTC of position | `hhmmss.ss` | no time | `time_utc` |
| 2 | Status | `A` = data valid, `V` = navigation receiver warning (no fix) | — | `status`, and `valid` = (`status` == `A`) |
| 3, 4 | Latitude, N/S | `ddmm.mmmm` | no position | `lat`, 6 decimals |
| 5, 6 | Longitude, E/W | `dddmm.mmmm` | no position | `lon`, 6 decimals |
| 7 | Speed over ground | knots | no value | `sog_knots`, 2 decimals |
| 8 | Course over ground | degrees true | no value (stationary receivers often leave it empty) | `cog_deg`, 2 decimals |
| 9 | Date | `ddmmyy` | no date | `date` as `"20yy-mm-dd"` |
| 10, 11 | Magnetic variation, E/W | degrees; `W` makes it negative | not computed | `magnetic_variation_deg`, 1 decimal |
| 12 | Mode indicator (NMEA 2.3+) | `A` autonomous, `D` differential, `E` estimated, `M` manual input, `S` simulator, `N` data not valid | older receiver | `mode` + `mode_text` |
| 13 | Navigational status (NMEA 4.10+) | `S` safe, `C` caution, `U` unsafe, `V` not valid | older receiver | (not output) |

`RMC` is the one sentence that carries both time *and* date, which is why
time servers use it. Speed over ground (SOG) and course over ground (COG)
are what the receiver measured from successive positions or Doppler, not
what a compass or log would say.

### 1.2.6 Log-file conventions in this book

The recordings in `sample-data/` — and the excerpts in `data/` — were made
with a small Python serial logger that writes a file of this shape:

```text
# START LOGGING UTC seconds since the epoch: 1197072047.98
# SPEED:       38400
# PORT:        /dev/tty.iBT-GPS-SPPslave-1
# TIMEOUT:     300.0
# STATIONID:   plane_to_sd
# DAEMON MODE: False
$GPGGA,000048.672,8960.0000,N,00000.0000,E,0,0,,137.0,M,13.0,M,,*4C,rplane_to_sd,1197072048.14
```

Three conventions matter to a parser:

1. **Comment lines** start with `#` and are not NMEA. The banner records
   the serial speed, the port, the station id and when logging started.
   A logger restart writes a new banner in the middle of the file
   (`sample-data/gps-itrek/gps.log.2007-12-08` has three). Our driver
   skips blank lines and lines starting with `#` and prints nothing for
   them.
2. **Two extra fields after the checksum**: `,r<station-id>,<unix-time>`.
   This is the US Coast Guard convention for tagging received sentences
   with the receiver that heard them and when
   [AIVDM "U.S. Coast Guard Extended AIVDM"], [AISUser §3.6.2.8], which the
   logger adopted for GPS data too. `rplane_to_sd` is `r` + the station id
   from the banner; `1197072048.14` is the logging computer's clock in UNIX
   seconds. The checksum does not cover these fields (§1.2.3). Our
   `parse_sentence` returns them separately as `after_checksum`, and
   chapter 5 decodes them into the `"uscg"` sub-object you will see in
   §1.5. Older files (2007) append only the timestamp; the oldest
   (`gps.log.2007-12-05.1`) put the time in a `#` comment line *before*
   each sentence instead.
3. **Line endings vary.** The standard says `\r\n`; receivers and
   terminal programs produce `\r\r\n`, `\n` alone, and occasionally a lone
   `\r`. The files in `data/` were normalised to `\n` when excerpted, but
   the raw logs were not. All four decoders read input with Python's
   "universal newlines" rule — `\n`, `\r\n` and a lone `\r` each end a
   line — and then strip whitespace from both ends, so a stray `\r` can
   neither hide inside a field nor glue two sentences together. Files are
   also frequently truncated mid-sentence where the logger was killed; the
   last line then fails its checksum and is reported, not silently
   dropped.

## 1.3 Decoding one sentence by hand

### 1.3.1 A GGA at cruising altitude

`data/ch01-gps-itrek-flight.nmea` is 33 lines from
`sample-data/gps-itrek/gps.log.2007-12-08`: lines 1–11 of the source (the
logger banner and the first cycle after power-up, with no fix) and lines
640–661 (the moment the receiver locks on, in an airliner over the
Four Corners region of the US Southwest). Line 17 of the excerpt (source
line 645):

```text
$GPGGA,000429.000,3601.5522,N,10947.9359,W,1,7,4.21,12491.0,M,-20.5,M,,*6D,rplane_to_sd,1197072268.78
```

Split at the commas. The address field is `GPGGA`: talker `GP`, formatter
`GGA`. Then fourteen data fields up to the `*`, the checksum, and two
logger fields:

| # | Text | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | `000429.000` | 00:04:29.000 UTC |
| 2, 3 | `3601.5522`, `N` | 36° 01.5522′ N = 36 + 1.5522/60 = **36.025870°** |
| 4, 5 | `10947.9359`, `W` | 109° 47.9359′ W = −(109 + 47.9359/60) = **−109.798932°** |
| 6 | `1` | GPS SPS fix |
| 7 | `7` | 7 satellites used |
| 8 | `4.21` | HDOP 4.21 — mediocre; the receiver sat on a seat-back tray inside a metal fuselage |
| 9, 10 | `12491.0`, `M` | 12 491.0 m above the geoid — about 41 000 ft, a normal cruising level |
| 11, 12 | `-20.5`, `M` | geoid is 20.5 m below the ellipsoid here, so the ellipsoidal height is 12 470.5 m |
| 13 | *(empty)* | no differential corrections |
| 14 | *(empty)* | no reference station |
| — | `*6D` | checksum; XOR of `GPGGA,000429.000,…,M,,` is 0x6D ✓ (exercise 1) |
| — | `rplane_to_sd` | logger station id |
| — | `1197072268.78` | logging computer's clock: 2007-12-08 00:04:28.78 UTC |

The last line is worth a moment: the GPS says the fix is for 00:04:29.000
and the laptop stamped the sentence's arrival at 00:04:28.78 — before the
fix time. The receiver cannot send a position before it has computed it,
so the laptop's clock was at least 0.22 s slow. Comparing the two clocks
over a whole file is exercise 6.

### 1.3.2 The RMC of the same second

Line 22 of the excerpt (source line 650):

```text
$GPRMC,000429.000,A,3601.5522,N,10947.9359,W,347.41,245.29,081207,,,A*7B,rplane_to_sd,1197072268.8
```

| # | Text | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | `000429.000` | 00:04:29.000 UTC — the same fix as the `GGA` |
| 2 | `A` | valid |
| 3–6 | `3601.5522,N,10947.9359,W` | 36.025870°, −109.798932° — identical to the `GGA` |
| 7 | `347.41` | 347.41 knots over the ground (643 km/h) |
| 8 | `245.29` | course 245.29° true: west-south-west, consistent with the station id `plane_to_sd` |
| 9 | `081207` | 8 December 2007 |
| 10, 11 | *(empty)*, *(empty)* | magnetic variation not computed |
| 12 | `A` | autonomous mode (no differential corrections) |
| — | `*7B` | checksum |
| — | `rplane_to_sd`, `1197072268.8` | logger fields; note the timestamp has one decimal here and two elsewhere — it is a printed float, not a fixed-width field |

One second later (lines 23 and 28) the aircraft is at 36.025237°,
−109.800743°: 0.00063° of latitude and 0.00181° of longitude further
WSW, which at this latitude is about 180 m — 347 knots is 178 m/s.
Arithmetic like this is how you check a parser against physics.

### 1.3.3 A receiver with nothing to say

`data/ch01-gps-itrek-coldstart.nmea` is lines 7–19 of
`sample-data/gps-itrek/log.2007-12-17`: the receiver being switched on in
Mountain View, California. After the six proprietary lines (§1.2.2) comes
a first cycle with no fix. Line 7 of the excerpt:

```text
$GPGGA,125258.827,3723.3433,N,12206.5233,W,0,0,,35.1,M,-25.7,M,,*4D,rkurt_itrek,1197895978.87
```

Fix quality `0`, zero satellites used, HDOP empty — and yet a position,
37.389055° N, 122.108722° W, and an altitude of 35.1 m. This is the

6. Lines 8–10 complete the picture: `$GPGSA,A,1,,,,,,,,,,,,,,,*1E` has

and course of `0.00`. One second later (line 13) the receiver already sees

no SNR yet.

## 1.4 The code

The `nmea` module exists four times —
[`code/python/aistut/nmea.py`](../code/python/aistut/nmea.py),
[`code/cpp/nmea.hpp`](../code/cpp/nmea.hpp),
[`code/rust/src/nmea.rs`](../code/rust/src/nmea.rs),
[`code/typescript/src/nmea.ts`](../code/typescript/src/nmea.ts) — and in
each it has the same parts:

* `checksum(body)` — the XOR of §1.2.3;
* `Sentence` — a record with `start`, `talker`, `formatter`, `fields`,
  `checksum_given`, `checksum_ok`, `after_checksum` and `raw`, plus an
  `address` accessor that re-joins talker and formatter;
* `parse_sentence(line)` — text in, `Sentence` out, or an `NmeaError`
  when the line is not a sentence at all;
* helpers `parse_latitude`, `parse_longitude`, `parse_utc_time`,
  `parse_date`, `opt_int`, `opt_float`, `fixed`;
* `parse_gga`, `parse_gsa`, `parse_gsv`, `parse_rmc` — each taking a
  `Sentence` and returning a JSON object — and a table `GPS_PARSERS` from
  formatter to function.

The driver (`decode`, chapter 2 §2.4) calls `parse_sentence`, looks the
formatter up in `GPS_PARSERS`, and prints the result with the line number
and the decoded logger fields. Below we quote the key functions of each
version; read the full files alongside.

### Python

```python
def checksum(body: str) -> int:
    """XOR of all characters in `body` (the text between '$' and '*').

    >>> hex(checksum("GPGSV,1,1,00"))
    '0x79'
    """
    value = 0
    for ch in body:
        value ^= ord(ch)
    return value
```

`ord(ch)` is the character's code point and `^=` is XOR-and-assign. The
parsed sentence is a `dataclass`:

```python
@dataclass
class Sentence:
    """One parsed NMEA sentence, before any formatter-specific decoding."""

    start: str                       # '$' or '!'
    talker: str                      # 'GP', 'AI', 'P' for proprietary ...
    formatter: str                   # 'GGA', 'VDM', 'MTK010' ...
    fields: list[str]                # data fields *after* the address field
    checksum_given: Optional[int]    # value written in the sentence, or None
    checksum_ok: bool                # True only if present and matching
    after_checksum: list[str] = field(default_factory=list)   # USCG extras
    raw: str = ""

    @property
    def address(self) -> str:
        """The address field, e.g. 'GPGGA' or 'AIVDM'."""
        return self.talker + self.formatter


class NmeaError(ValueError):
    """Raised for text that is not an NMEA sentence at all."""
```

and `parse_sentence` does the splitting:

```python
def parse_sentence(line: str) -> Sentence:
    line = line.strip()
    start_at = min((i for i in (line.find("$"), line.find("!")) if i >= 0), default=-1)
    if start_at < 0:
        raise NmeaError("no '$' or '!' in line")
    line = line[start_at:]
    start = line[0]

    body, star, tail = line[1:].partition("*")
    given: Optional[int] = None
    ok = False
    extras: list[str] = []
    if star:
        hexpart, _, rest = tail.partition(",")
        try:
            given = int(hexpart[:2], 16)
        except ValueError:
            given = None
        ok = given is not None and given == checksum(body)
        if rest:
            extras = rest.split(",")
    # If there was no '*' we still parse the fields but checksum_ok is False.

    address, *fields = body.split(",")
    if len(address) < 3:
        raise NmeaError(f"address field too short: {address!r}")
    if address.startswith("P"):          # proprietary: P + 3-char maker code
        talker, formatter = "P", address[1:]
    else:
        talker, formatter = address[:2], address[2:]
    return Sentence(start, talker, formatter, fields, given, ok, extras, line)
```

The structure follows §1.2.1 line by line: find the start delimiter
(skipping any prefix such as the `file.log:` that `grep` adds), cut at
`*`, read two hex digits, compare with the XOR of the body, split the
remainder of the tail at commas into the logger extras, split the body at
commas, and separate talker from formatter. A line without `*` is still
split into fields — the driver decides what to do about the missing
checksum (it reports `no checksum`, §1.5).

> **Language notes (Python).** A **function** is introduced with `def`,
> its body is indented, and `return` hands back a value. The **type
> hints** `body: str` and `-> int` document (and let tools check) what
> goes in and out; Python does not enforce them at run time. `list[str]`
> is a list of strings and `Optional[int]` means "an `int` or `None`" —
> `None` is Python's "no value". A **list** (`[]`, `.append`, `len`) is an
> ordered, growable sequence; a **dict** (`{"key": value}`, `.get(key,
> default)`) maps keys to values and is how we build JSON objects.
> **Slicing** `s[2:]`, `s[:2]`, `s[i:i+4]` takes a sub-sequence from a
> string or list (start inclusive, end exclusive, either may be omitted).
> `a, b, c = x.partition("*")` **unpacks** a three-item result into three
> names; `address, *fields = …` puts the first item in `address` and the
> rest in a list. An **f-string** `f"…{address!r}…"` interpolates values
> into text (`!r` uses the `repr`, which adds quotes). A **`@dataclass`**
> turns a class whose body is only typed field names into a record with an
> automatic constructor, so `Sentence(start, talker, …)` works;
> `field(default_factory=list)` gives each instance its own empty list.
> `@property` makes `s.address` look like a field while being computed.
> **Exceptions**: `raise NmeaError(...)` abandons the function and every
> caller until a `try:`/`except NmeaError:` catches it; defining
> `class NmeaError(ValueError)` makes a new exception type that is also a
> `ValueError`. `try: int(x, 16) except ValueError:` is how we turn a
> malformed hex pair into `None` instead of a crash. Finally, **modules**:
> `nmea.py` is a module in the package `aistut` (the directory with
> `__init__.py`); `from .jsonout import Fixed` imports one name from a
> sibling module, and `decode.py` does `from aistut.nmea import
> parse_sentence`.

```cpp
inline Sentence parse_sentence(std::string_view input) {
    std::string line = py::strip(input);

    // Start at the first '$' or '!', whichever comes first.
    std::size_t dollar = line.find('$');
    std::size_t bang = line.find('!');
    std::size_t start_at = std::min(dollar, bang);   // npos is the largest value
    if (start_at == std::string::npos) {
        throw NmeaError("no '$' or '!' in line");
    }
    line = line.substr(start_at);
    char start = line[0];

    py::Partition at_star = py::partition(std::string_view(line).substr(1), '*');
    const std::string& body = at_star.head;
    std::optional<int> given;
    bool ok = false;
    std::vector<std::string> extras;
    if (at_star.found) {
        py::Partition at_comma = py::partition(at_star.tail, ',');
        std::optional<long long> parsed = py::to_int(py::slice(at_comma.head, 0, 2), 16);
        if (parsed) given = static_cast<int>(*parsed);
        ok = given.has_value() && *given == checksum(body);
        if (at_comma.found && !at_comma.tail.empty()) {
            extras = py::split(at_comma.tail, ',');
        }
    }
    // If there was no '*' we still parse the fields but checksum_ok is false.

    std::vector<std::string> parts = py::split(body, ',');
    std::string address = parts[0];
    std::vector<std::string> fields(parts.begin() + 1, parts.end());
    if (address.size() < 3) {
        throw NmeaError("address field too short: " + py::repr(address));
    }
    Sentence s;
    s.start = start;
    if (address[0] == 'P') {                  // proprietary: P + 3-char maker code
        s.talker = "P";
        s.formatter = address.substr(1);
    } else {
        s.talker = address.substr(0, 2);
        s.formatter = address.substr(2);
    }
    s.fields = std::move(fields);
    s.checksum_given = given;
    s.checksum_ok = ok;
    s.after_checksum = std::move(extras);
    s.raw = line;
    return s;
}
```

Where Python wrote `int(hexpart[:2], 16)` inside a `try`, C++ uses

throwing — a pattern you will see throughout the C++ code for routine,

`Json` is the tutorial's own value type (chapter 2); `Json::Object` is a

> **Language notes (C++).** `#include <string>` and friends pull in the
> standard library piece by piece; `#pragma once` keeps a header from being
> included twice. This whole decoder is **header-only**: every function is
> marked `inline` so that it may be defined in a header, and `decode.cpp`
> is the only file the compiler is given. `std::string` **owns** its
> characters; `std::string_view` is a borrowed (pointer, length) pair over
> someone else's characters — cheap to pass, but it must not outlive the
> string it looks into, which is why `parse_sentence` copies into a
> `std::string line` first. `std::vector<T>` is the growable array
> (Python's list). A **`struct`** is a bundle of named members with no
> hidden behaviour; `= '$'` and `= false` are default member values, and
> because `Sentence` has no constructor you create one with `Sentence s;`
> and fill it in. **`const`** after a method (`address() const`) promises
> the method does not modify the object; `const std::string&` as a
> parameter type is a **reference** — the function sees the caller's
> string without copying it and may not change it. Pass by reference for
> anything bigger than a number; `std::move(fields)` hands the vector's
> contents over without a copy when the source is not needed again.
> **`std::optional<T>`** is "a T or nothing": `std::nullopt` is nothing,
> `if (quality)` tests presence, `*quality` reads the value. It replaces
> both Python's `None` and the "return −1 on error" convention.
> **Exceptions**: `throw NmeaError(...)` unwinds to the nearest matching
> `catch`; `class NmeaError : public std::invalid_argument` derives a new
> exception type, and `explicit` on its constructor stops the compiler from
> silently converting a string into an error. `static_cast<int>(x)` is an
> explicit numeric conversion; `auto it = …` lets the compiler write the
> (long) type of a map iterator for you. `std::size_t` is the unsigned
> type for sizes, and `std::string::npos` ("no position") is its maximum
> value, which is why `std::min(dollar, bang)` finds the earliest
> delimiter that exists.

There are no exceptions in Rust, so `parse_sentence` returns a
`Result<Sentence, NmeaError>` — either `Ok(sentence)` or `Err(error)`:

```rust
pub fn parse_sentence(line: &str) -> Result<Sentence, NmeaError> {
    let line = py_strip(line);
    // Python: min(i for i in (find('$'), find('!')) if i >= 0). Both finds
    // return byte offsets of ASCII characters, so slicing at them is safe.
    let start_at = match (line.find('$'), line.find('!')) {
        (Some(a), Some(b)) => a.min(b),
        (Some(a), None) => a,
        (None, Some(b)) => b,
        (None, None) => return Err(NmeaError::NoStart),
    };
    let line = &line[start_at..];
    let start = if line.starts_with('$') { '$' } else { '!' };

    // Python: body, star, tail = line[1:].partition("*")
    let after_start = &line[1..];
    let mut given: Option<i64> = None;
    let mut ok = false;
    let mut extras: Vec<String> = Vec::new();
    let body = match after_start.split_once('*') {
        Some((body, tail)) => {
            // Python: hexpart, _, rest = tail.partition(",")
            let (hexpart, rest) = match tail.split_once(',') {
                Some((h, r)) => (h, r),
                None => (tail, ""),
            };
            let first_two: String = hexpart.chars().take(2).collect();
            given = py_int_radix(&first_two, 16);
            ok = given == Some(i64::from(checksum(body)));
            if !rest.is_empty() {
                extras = rest.split(',').map(str::to_string).collect();
            }
            body
        }
        // If there was no '*' we still parse the fields but checksum_ok is false.
        None => after_start,
    };

    // Python: address, *fields = body.split(",")
    let mut parts = body.split(',');
    let address = parts.next().unwrap_or("");
    let fields: Vec<String> = parts.map(str::to_string).collect();
    if address.chars().count() < 3 {
        return Err(NmeaError::AddressTooShort(address.to_string()));
    }
    let (talker, formatter) = match address.strip_prefix('P') {
        Some(rest) => ("P".to_string(), rest.to_string()), // proprietary
        None => {
            // The first two *characters* (not bytes) are the talker.
            let split_at = address
                .char_indices()
                .nth(2)
                .map(|(i, _)| i)
                .unwrap_or(address.len());
            (address[..split_at].to_string(), address[split_at..].to_string())
        }
    };
    Ok(Sentence {
        start,
        talker,
        formatter,
        fields,
        checksum_given: given,
        checksum_ok: ok,
        after_checksum: extras,
        raw: line.to_string(),
    })
}
```

> **function**; the last expression without a semicolon is the return
> value (`value` at the end of `checksum`). `let` binds an immutable name;
> `let mut` one you may change. `pub` makes an item visible outside the
> **module** (`nmea.rs` is `mod nmea` in `lib.rs`; `use crate::jsonout::…`
> imports from a sibling). **Ownership and borrowing**: a `String` owns
> its text and is freed when its owner goes out of scope; a `&str` is a
> borrowed view into text owned by someone else. Functions that only read
> take `&str`; a struct that must outlive the input line stores `String`
> (`.to_string()` makes the copy). `&self` in a method borrows the struct;
> `&f[5]` borrows one element. **`Option<T>`** is `Some(value)` or
> `None`; **`Result<T, E>`** is `Ok(value)` or `Err(error)`. The `?`
> operator after a `Result` means "if this is `Err`, return it from the
> current function right now; otherwise unwrap the `Ok`" — it is Rust's
> replacement for exceptions and it is why `parse_gga` reads like straight
> code. **`match`** compares a value against patterns in order and must
> cover every case (`_` is the catch-all); matching on a tuple
> `(line.find('$'), line.find('!'))` handles four combinations in four
> lines. **`struct`** declares a record and `impl Sentence { … }` attaches
> methods; `#[derive(Debug, Clone, PartialEq)]` asks the compiler to write
> printing, copying and comparison for you. An **`enum`** lists
> alternatives, and each may carry data (`AddressTooShort(String)`) —
> `NmeaError` is a sum type, not a list of integers. `impl fmt::Display for
> NmeaError` is where the error's text lives, so `e.to_string()` works.
> `Vec<String>` is the growable array; `.iter().map(…).collect()` builds
> one from an iterator. Strings are UTF-8 and may only be sliced on
> character boundaries, hence `chars().count()`, `char_indices()` and
> `split_once` instead of numeric indexing; `|(i, _)| i` is a closure (an
> anonymous function). `&'static str` is a string literal that lives for
> the whole program, which is what a lookup table returns.

### TypeScript

`nmea.ts` is compiled by `tsc` to plain JavaScript and run by `node`.
The checksum:

```typescript
export function checksum(body: string): number {
  let value = 0;
  for (const ch of body) {
    value ^= ch.codePointAt(0) ?? 0;
  }
  return value;
}
```

The record is a class with `readonly` constructor parameters, and the
error is a subclass of the tutorial's `ValueError`:

```typescript
export class Sentence {
  constructor(
    readonly start: string,                  // '$' or '!'
    readonly talker: string,                 // 'GP', 'AI', 'P' for proprietary ...
    readonly formatter: string,              // 'GGA', 'VDM', 'MTK010' ...
    readonly fields: readonly string[],      // data fields *after* the address field
    readonly checksumGiven: number | null,   // value written in the sentence, or null
    readonly checksumOk: boolean,            // true only if present and matching
    readonly afterChecksum: readonly string[] = [],   // USCG extras
    readonly raw: string = "",
  ) {}

  /** The address field, e.g. 'GPGGA' or 'AIVDM'. */
  get address(): string {
    return this.talker + this.formatter;
  }
}

export class NmeaError extends ValueError {
  constructor(message: string) {
    super(message);
    this.name = "NmeaError";
  }
}
```

`parseSentence` follows the Python original, with `partition`, `pyStrip`
and `pyHexInt` helpers from `common.ts` that copy Python's acceptance
rules:

```typescript
export function parseSentence(rawLine: string): Sentence {
  let line = pyStrip(rawLine);
  // The earliest of '$' and '!' that is actually present.
  const candidates = [line.indexOf("$"), line.indexOf("!")].filter((i) => i >= 0);
  if (candidates.length === 0) {
    throw new NmeaError("no '$' or '!' in line");
  }
  line = line.slice(Math.min(...candidates));
  const start = line[0];

  const [body, star, tail] = partition(line.slice(1), "*");
  let given: number | null = null;
  let ok = false;
  let extras: string[] = [];
  if (star) {
    const [hexpart, , rest] = partition(tail, ",");
    try {
      given = pyHexInt(hexpart.slice(0, 2));
    } catch (e) {
      if (!(e instanceof ValueError)) {
        throw e;
      }
      given = null;
    }
    ok = given !== null && given === checksum(body);
    if (rest) {
      extras = rest.split(",");
    }
  }
  // If there was no '*' we still parse the fields but checksumOk is false.

  const [address, ...fields] = body.split(",");
  if (address.length < 3) {
    throw new NmeaError(`address field too short: ${pyRepr(address)}`);
  }
  let talker: string;
  let formatter: string;
  if (address.startsWith("P")) { // proprietary: P + 3-char maker code
    talker = "P";
    formatter = address.slice(1);
  } else {
    talker = address.slice(0, 2);
    formatter = address.slice(2);
  }
  return new Sentence(start, talker, formatter, fields, given, ok, extras, line);
}
```

Latitude and `GGA`:

```typescript
export function parseLatitude(ddmm: string, hemisphere: string): number | null {
  if (!ddmm) {
    return null;
  }
  const degrees = pyInt(ddmm.slice(0, 2));
  const minutes = pyFloat(ddmm.slice(2));
  const value = degrees + minutes / 60.0;
  return hemisphere === "S" ? -value : value;
}

export function parseGga(s: Sentence): JsonObject {
  const f = padFields(s.fields, 14);
  const quality = optInt(f[5]);
  return {
    sentence: "GGA",
    time_utc: parseUtcTime(f[0]),
    lat: fixed(parseLatitude(f[1], f[2]), 6),
    lon: fixed(parseLongitude(f[3], f[4]), 6),
    fix_quality: quality,
    fix_quality_text: quality !== null ? (GGA_FIX_QUALITY[quality] ?? "unknown") : null,
    satellites_used: optInt(f[6]),
    hdop: fixed(optFloat(f[7]), 2),
    altitude_m: fixed(optFloat(f[8]), 1),
    geoid_separation_m: fixed(optFloat(f[10]), 1),
    dgps_age_s: fixed(optFloat(f[12]), 1),
    dgps_station: f[13] || null,
  };
}
```

Two details are specific to JavaScript. `pyInt` and `pyFloat` exist
because the built-ins are too forgiving: `Number("")` is `0` and
`parseInt("12abc")` is `12`, whereas Python (and the output contract)
reject both. And the parser table is a `Map`, not a plain object:

```typescript
export const GPS_PARSERS: ReadonlyMap<string, GpsParser> = new Map([
  ["GGA", parseGga], ["GSA", parseGsa], ["GSV", parseGsv], ["RMC", parseRmc],
]);
```

because a plain object inherits keys such as `"constructor"` from
`Object.prototype`, and the formatter string comes from the input.

> **Language notes (TypeScript).** TypeScript is JavaScript plus static
> types; `tsc` checks the types and emits JavaScript with them erased.
> `function name(arg: string): number` declares parameter and return
> types. `const` binds a name that cannot be reassigned, `let` one that
> can. **Arrow functions** `(i) => i >= 0` are short anonymous functions,
> used here as the predicate of `.filter`. **Union types** such as
> `number | null` say a value may be either; the compiler refuses
> `given + 1` until you have checked `given !== null`. We use `null` (not
> `undefined`) for "no data" because JSON has `null`. `??` supplies a
> default only for `null`/`undefined`; `||` also replaces `""` and `0`,
> which is exactly Python's `f[13] or None`. **Template literals** in
> back-ticks interpolate `${expressions}` — the f-string. **`class`
> versus `interface`**: `Sentence` is a class because it has behaviour
> (the `address` getter) and because `instanceof` works on classes; a pure
> data shape would be an `interface`, which exists only at compile time
> (chapter 2's `Vdm`). Declaring constructor parameters with
> **`readonly`** both creates the fields and forbids later assignment;
> `readonly string[]` is an array you may read but not `push` to.
> `extends ValueError` plus `super(message)` makes a new error class;
> `catch (e)` receives *anything* that was thrown, so the code tests
> `instanceof ValueError` and re-throws everything else. **Destructuring**
> `const [body, star, tail] = partition(…)` unpacks an array and
> `const [address, ...fields] = …` collects the rest. **Modules**: each
> file is a module, `export` publishes a name, `import { Fixed } from
> "./jsonout"` consumes one; `tsconfig.json` sets `strict` so that
> `null` checks are enforced.

### Where the four differ

* **Missing values.** Python `None`, C++ `std::optional`, Rust `Option`,
  TypeScript `null` in a union type. Only Python lets you forget to check.
* **Errors.** Python and TypeScript throw; C++ throws for "not a
  sentence" but returns an empty optional for a bad hex pair; Rust
  returns `Result` everywhere and uses `?` to propagate. The Rust version
  is the only one that turns a non-numeric field into a reported line
  error rather than a crash, and it does so using Python's error text so
  that the outputs still match.
* **Strings.** Python and TypeScript index by character; C++ by byte;
  Rust by byte but only on character boundaries, which forces
  `char_indices()` where the others write `[:2]`.
* **Lookup tables.** Python `dict`, C++ `const std::map`, Rust `match`,
  TypeScript a plain object for integer keys and a `Map` for string keys.
* **Sorted JSON keys.** C++ and Rust get them from `std::map`/`BTreeMap`;
  Python and TypeScript sort in their JSON writers.

## 1.6 Pitfalls and real-world deviations

* **89°60′ north.** The i.Trek's MediaTek chipset fills the position
  fields with `8960.0000,N,00000.0000,E` while it has no fix (lines 7 and
  11 of the flight file). Sixty minutes is not a legal minute value — it
  is 90° written illegally — and our parser, like most, happily computes
  90.000000. Other receivers emit `0000.0000,N,00000.0000,E` (a point in
  the Gulf of Guinea, where web maps show a steady stream of "ships"), or
  leave the fields empty, or repeat the last fix (the cold-start file,
  §1.3.3). The only defence is to **check `fix_quality` (GGA) or
  `status`/`mode` (RMC) before using a position**, and to treat minutes
  ≥ 60 as an error (exercise 4).
* **Altitude is not ellipsoidal height.** GGA field 9 is height above
  the geoid; add field 11 to get height above the WGS-84 ellipsoid
  [IEC 61162-1 §8 (GGA)]. Mixing the two is a 20.5 m error in our data
  and up to ±100 m elsewhere in the world. Software that compares GPS
  altitude with a digital elevation model, a barometric altimeter or an
  AIS message 9 aircraft altitude (chapter 10) must know which it has.
* **The last GSV sentence is short.** A group of 3 sentences for 9
  satellites ends with `$GPGSV,3,3,09,02,,,18*7B` — three data fields
  plus one satellite, not four. Code that assumes 19 fields reads past
  the end. Our `parse_gsv` loops while four fields remain, and the Python
  `range(3, len(f) - 3, 4)` bound, the C++ `i + 4 <= f.size()`, the Rust
  `chunks_exact(4)` and the TypeScript `i < f.length - 3` all express the
  same rule. NMEA 4.11 then appended a *signal id* field after the last
  satellite [NMEA 0183], so a modern receiver's GSV has 4n + 4 fields;
  the "four at a time" loop silently ignores the extra one.
* **Two-digit years.** `081207` is 8 December 2007 only if you supply the
  century. `parse_date` assumes 2000–2099. A receiver whose almanac is
  stale, or whose firmware predates the GPS week rollovers of 1999 and
  2019, can report a date 1 024 weeks (19.6 years) in the past; such
  receivers produced RMC dates in 1999 and 2000 during 2019. If a logged
  date disagrees with the logger's own timestamp by about two decades,
  that is why.
* **The checksum covers only up to `*`.** Feeding the whole line — or the
  whole line minus `$` — to the XOR fails on every sentence in our logs
  because of the `,r…,…` suffix, and fails on perfectly standard sentences
  if the CR LF is included. Conversely, `parse_sentence` reads exactly two
  hex digits after `*` and ignores whatever follows; some receivers emit
  lower-case hex (`*7b`), which `int(…, 16)` accepts.
* **Line endings and lone `\r`.** Terminal captures and some loggers
  produce `\r\r\n`; a few devices send a bare `\r`. If your reader splits
  only on `\n` the stray `\r` ends up inside the last field (making
  `"A\r"` fail the `== "A"` test, or `"1197072268.78\r"` fail to parse)
  or, worse, two sentences share one line and the second is lost. All
  four decoders use universal-newline splitting and strip both ends; do
  the same in your own code before you do anything else.
* **The field count is not fixed for ever.** `RMC` had 11 data fields in
  NMEA 2.0, 12 from 2.3 and 13 from 4.10 [NMEA 0183]; a 2007 receiver
  writes 12. Pad to the longest known layout and read by position; never
  reject a sentence for having too few or too many fields.
* **Time is not from the computer.** The GPS time in the sentence and the
  logger's timestamp after it come from different clocks (§1.3.1). Use
  the GPS fields for *when the fix was*, the logger's for *when it was
  received*; the difference is latency plus clock error, and it is
  negative on line 17 because the laptop was slow.
* **Trusting `GP`.** A modern receiver emits `GN` talkers for combined
  solutions and `GL`/`GA`/`GB` for per-constellation `GSV` and `GSA`
  sentences. Our driver dispatches on the formatter and ignores the
  talker for GPS sentences, which is the right choice; code that matches
  the string `"$GPGGA"` stops working on new hardware.
