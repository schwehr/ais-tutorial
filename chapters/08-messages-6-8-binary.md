# 8. Messages 6 and 8: binary messages, application identifiers and the BBM/ABM/ABK sentences

> **What you will learn**
> * Why AIS has two "containers" for arbitrary binary data — message 6 (addressed, acknowledged by message 7) and message 8 (broadcast) — and what their 88- and 56-bit headers contain.
> * How the 16-bit **application identifier** (a 10-bit designated area code, DAC, and a 6-bit function identifier, FI) tells a receiver which authority's table describes the bits that follow, and why DAC 366 means "USA", 1 "international" and 0 "test".
> * Why the number of data bits you receive is not necessarily the number the application sent: the 5-slot limit, Tables 55/58, and byte-boundary padding.
> * The *sending* side, which most tutorials skip: how an application hands binary data to a transceiver with `!--BBM` / `!--ABM`, what the `$--ABK` acknowledgement types 0–4 mean, and a complete recorded send path — a project-internal `BMS,…` request, the `!VTBBM` built from it, the transceiver's `!AIVDO` echo, the `$AIABK`, and the same message heard by an independent receiver.
> * Language notes: a registry of decoder functions keyed by `(dac, fi)`; optional sub-objects; `std::optional<Json>`, `Option<Json>`, `JsonObject | null`.

## 8.1 Where this fits

Every message in chapters 3–7 has a fixed meaning written into ITU-R M.1371. Messages 6 and 8 do not. They were designed in 1998 as general-purpose envelopes: a short header, a 16-bit label saying *whose* format follows, and up to 920 or 952 bits of "application specific data" that M.1371 deliberately leaves undefined [M.1371-5 Annex 8 §3.4 Table 54, §3.6 Table 57]. The idea was that port authorities, national administrations and IMO would define their own **application-specific messages** (ASMs) — water levels, lock schedules, weather, hazard areas — without having to change the ITU recommendation each time [M.1371-5 Annex 5 §1, §2].

* **Message 6, addressed binary message**, goes from one station to one other station. The receiver's link layer answers with a **message 7** (chapter 12), and the sender retries up to three times, 4 s apart, if no acknowledgement comes back [M.1371-5 Annex 8 §3.4; Annex 2 §5.3.1]. Priority 4, access schemes RATDMA/FATDMA/ITDMA (random, fixed and incremental access TDMA; chapter 3), sent by mobile *or* base stations [M.1371-5 Annex 8 Table 46].
* **Message 8, binary broadcast message**, goes to everyone in range and is never acknowledged. Same priority and access schemes [M.1371-5 Annex 8 Table 46].

Both may occupy 1–5 slots, "up to 3 slots, or up to 5 slots when able to use FATDMA reservations"; Class B "SO" stations are limited to 3 slots and Class B "CS" stations must not transmit them at all [M.1371-5 Table 54, Table 57]. A Class B "CS" unit need not even receive message 6 (Table 42 says "No") and may optionally process message 8 [M.1371-5 Annex 7 Table 42].

Two later envelopes, messages 25 and 26, were added in 2007 with the same DAC/FI idea but lighter headers; chapter 16 covers them. This chapter is about the containers and the sentence-level plumbing; chapter 9 opens the containers and decodes what is inside.

### Operational use

* **Shore to ship, structured.** A vessel traffic service (VTS) or port can push a water level, a lock order or a hazard area to the ships in its area as a message 8, or to one ship as a message 6. The St. Lawrence Seaway has done this since the early 2000s with its own DAC 316/366 family [Seaway R41]; IMO published international sets in 2004 and 2010 [Circ.236; Circ.289]. On the bridge the result is only as good as the display software: an ECDIS that does not know the DAC/FI shows nothing, which is why IMO issued separate presentation guidance [Circ.290].
* **Ship to shore, structured.** The same containers carry a ship's answers: number of persons on board, dangerous cargo, ETA at a lock [Circ.289; AIVDM "Type 6"].
* **Text.** The ITU-defined *international function message 0* (DAC 1 FI 0) is a text telegram in six-bit ASCII inside a message 6 or 8 [M.1371-5 Annex 5 §5.1 Tables 25/26] — a second text channel next to messages 12/14, used for application-to-application text rather than safety messages.

### Unattended / automated uses

The binary containers exist *for* machines, and almost everything you will see in them was generated by a program and consumed (if at all) by another program:

* **Environmental sensor networks.** The 2006 UNH experiments in `sample-data/tide1` put a tide gauge's water level into message 8 so that a survey launch could read it over the air [UNH RFC]. NOAA PORTS stations, the Seaway's weather stations and the IMO met/hydro messages (chapter 9) are the same idea at scale: a sensor, a formatter, a transceiver, no human in the loop.
* **Hazard notices generated from data.** The right-whale notices in `sample-data/RightwhaleAisProject` were produced every few minutes by a script that read acoustic-detection results from ten buoys and encoded a "whales present / not observed" circle per buoy into a message 8 [AtoN form v4; RTCM SC121]. This chapter shows the complete machine path from request to reception.
* **Encrypted tracking.** The USCG's encrypted AIS puts a ship's position and static data into messages 8 with DAC 366 FI 56/57, unreadable without the key [EAIS IDD]. To an ordinary decoder they are the most common binary messages in US waters in 2008–2011 (13 108 FI 56 and 9 714 FI 57 in one day of `RightwhaleAisProject/20081013.log`).
* **Test traffic.** DAC 0 is reserved for testing and "the function identifier is arbitrary" [M.1371-5 Annex 5 §2.1.1]. Bench tests, new installations and misconfigured units broadcast DAC 0 — or, as §8.6 shows, DACs that mean nothing at all.
* **What can go wrong without anyone noticing.** A broadcast is fire-and-forget: there is no delivery guarantee, no receipt, and a 5-slot message is more likely to be lost to a collision than a 1-slot one [Circ.289 §11.7]. An addressed message *is* acknowledged, but "these acknowledgements are applicable only to the VHF data link. Other means must be employed for acknowledging applications" [M.1371-5 Annex 8 §3.5; Annex 2 §5.3.1] — the radio got it, nobody knows if the application did. §8.6 shows two base stations addressing a maritime mobile service identity (MMSI) that cannot exist.
* **Anomaly and spoofing detection.** Message 6/8 traffic is a good place to look for mis-programmed equipment: a DAC that is not a valid MID, a spare bit set, a source MMSI with one bit set, a payload shorter than its own header. All of these occur in our recordings and all are flagged by the decoder's output.

## 8.2 The specification

### Message 6: addressed binary message

| Bits | Len | Field | Type/units | Not-available | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0–5 | 6 | Message ID | uint | — | 6 [M.1371-5 §3.4 Table 54] |
| 6–7 | 2 | Repeat indicator | uint | — | 0 default, 3 = do not repeat [Table 54; Annex 2 §4.6.1] |
| 8–37 | 30 | Source ID | MMSI | — | [Table 54] |
| 38–39 | 2 | Sequence number | uint 0–3 | — | chosen by the application; echoed in message 7 [Table 54; Annex 2 §5.3.1] |
| 40–69 | 30 | Destination ID | MMSI | — | [Table 54] |
| 70 | 1 | Retransmit flag | flag | 0 | "should be set upon retransmission: 0 = no retransmission = default; 1 = retransmitted" [Table 54] |
| 71 | 1 | Spare | — | 0 | [Table 54] |
| 72–81 | 10 | DAC | uint | — | designated area code [Table 54; Annex 5 §2.1 Table 23] |
| 82–87 | 6 | FI | uint | — | function identifier [Table 54; Annex 5 Table 23] |
| 88– | ≤ 920 | Application data | bits | — | "application specific data" [Table 54] |

Total 88 to 1 008 bits [Table 54]. The 16-bit DAC/FI pair and the data together are what Table 54 calls "binary data", maximum 936 bits.

### Message 8: binary broadcast message

| Bits | Len | Field | Type/units | Not-available | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0–5 | 6 | Message ID | uint | — | 8 [M.1371-5 §3.6 Table 57] |
| 6–7 | 2 | Repeat indicator | uint | — | [Table 57] |
| 8–37 | 30 | Source ID | MMSI | — | [Table 57] |
| 38–39 | 2 | Spare | — | 0 | [Table 57] |
| 40–49 | 10 | DAC | uint | — | [Table 57; Annex 5 Table 23] |
| 50–55 | 6 | FI | uint | — | [Table 57; Annex 5 Table 23] |
| 56– | ≤ 952 | Application data | bits | — | [Table 57] |

Total 56 to 1 008 bits [Table 57]. There is no sequence number, no destination and no retransmit flag: a broadcast has nothing to acknowledge.

### The application identifier

Annex 5 defines the 16 bits that both messages place in front of the data [M.1371-5 Annex 5 §2.1 Table 23]:

| Bits (of the 16) | Field | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| 15–6 (10 bits) | DAC, designated area code | "based on the maritime identification digits (MID). Exceptions are 0 (test) and 1 (international)". Codes ≥ 1 000 reserved. |
| 5–0 (6 bits) | FI, function identifier | "The meaning should be determined by the authority which is responsible for the area given in the designated area code." |

So the DAC says *who owns the table*, and the FI is an index into that owner's table of up to 64 messages. Some DACs you will meet:

| DAC | Owner | Where defined |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | test; "any function identifier (0 to 63)… The function identifier is arbitrary" | [M.1371-5 Annex 5 §2.1.1] |
| 1 | international: FI 0–5 are ITU system functions (text telegram, interrogation, capability, application acknowledgement), FI 10–63 "reserved for international operational applications" and allocated by IMO | [M.1371-5 Annex 5 §2.1.2 Table 24; Circ.236; Circ.289] |
| 200 | inland waterways (Europe); the MID block 200 is otherwise unused | [AIVDM "Inland AIS"] |
| 235, 250 | UK, Ireland: AtoN monitoring | [AIVDM "Type 6"] |
| 316 | Canada — St. Lawrence Seaway messages sent from the Canadian side | [Seaway R41 §1.1] |
| 366, 367 | USA — Seaway/PAWSS from the US side, whale notices, encrypted AIS | [Seaway R41 §1.1; AtoN form v4; EAIS IDD] |

Three consequences for a decoder. First, **a DAC/FI is a label, not a guarantee**: nothing stops a broken transmitter from sending DAC 1 FI 31 followed by garbage, so "decoders should perform range validation on the structured fields and interpret the message as unstructured if any check fails" [AIVDM "Type 8"]. Second, the *same* FI means different things under different DACs, and the same DAC/FI may be defined separately for message 6 and message 8 ("DAC/FID pairs are assigned separately per message type" [AIVDM "Type 6"]). Third, there is no on-air version number: when IMO replaced the 2004 met/hydro message (FI 11) with a different layout in 2010 it had to give it a new FI (31) [Circ.289 Table 1].

### How many bits fit — Tables 55 and 58

A slot carries 256 bits, of which the data part is 168 bits for a one-slot message; multi-slot messages add 224 bits per extra slot but must also allow for *bit stuffing*, the HDLC rule that inserts a 0 after every five consecutive 1s [M.1371-5 Annex 2 §3.2.2.1, §5.2.1 Table 21]. Because stuffing depends on the data, M.1371 gives conservative byte budgets:

| Slots | Message 6: max binary data bytes [Table 55] | Message 8: max binary data bytes [Table 58] |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 8 | 12 |
| 2 | 36 | 40 |
| 3 | 64 | 68 |
| 4 | 92 | 96 |
| 5 | 117 | 121 |

"Binary data" here includes the 2-byte application identifier. So a one-slot message 8 can carry 12 × 8 − 16 = **80 application data bits** with a guaranteed fit, and a one-slot message 6 only 48. The whale notice of §8.3 uses 112 data bits (128 with DAC/FI = 16 bytes) and so needs two slots.

### Byte alignment and padding

Annex 2 says, in one easily missed sentence, "unused bits in the last byte should be set to zero in order to preserve byte boundary" [M.1371-5 Annex 2 §3.3.7], and Annex 5 adds that "all data fields of the functional messages should observe byte boundaries. If needed to align with byte boundaries, spares should be inserted" [M.1371-5 Annex 5 §4]. The transceiver, not the application, pads the frame; the receiving transceiver may or may not strip the padding before it builds the `!AIVDM` sentence. The practical result is that `data_bits` in our output can be up to 7 bits longer than the application's table says, and a decoder must treat trailing zero bits as noise, not as a format error [AIVDM "Byte Alignment"; Toils]. §8.6 shows a case where the padding is a full byte.

### Sequence numbers, retries and message 7

Each addressed packet "should have a unique packet identifier consisting of the message type (binary or safety related messages), the source-ID, the destination-ID, and a sequence number"; the destination returns the same sequence number in its message 7; the sender "should not reuse a sequence number until it has been acknowledged or time-out has occurred" [M.1371-5 Annex 2 §5.3.1]. If no message 7 arrives, "the station should wait 4 s before attempting retries. When a transmission is retried, the retransmit flag should be set to retransmitted. The number of retries should be 3" [M.1371-5 Annex 2 §5.3.1]. Message 7 itself — 40 header bits plus up to four 32-bit (MMSI, sequence number) pairs — is decoded in chapter 12 by `msg_7_13`; we do not repeat it here.

### The presentation interface: ABM, BBM and ABK

How does an application get bits *into* a message 6 or 8? Through three IEC 61162-1 sentences on the transceiver's serial port [IEC 61162-1 §8 ABM, BBM, ABK]:

| Sentence | Direction | Fields |
|---|---|---|
| `!--ABM,t,n,s,dddddddddd,c,m,data,f*hh` | application → transceiver | total sentences `t`, sentence number `n`, sequential message identifier `s` (0–3), destination MMSI, AIS channel `c` (0 = no preference, 1 = A, 2 = B, 3 = both), message ID `m` (6 or 12), six-bit armoured data, fill bits `f` (0–5). Produces a message 6 (or 12). |
| `!--BBM,t,n,s,c,m,data,f*hh` | application → transceiver | as ABM but no destination; `s` is 0–9; message ID 8 (or 14). Produces a message 8 (or 14). |
| `$--ABK,dddddddddd,c,m,s,a*hh` | transceiver → application | destination MMSI (empty for broadcasts), channel of reception, message ID, sequence number, acknowledgement type `a`: **0** = message 6/12 acknowledged by the addressed unit (message 7/13 received), **1** = message was broadcast but not acknowledged, **2** = message could not be broadcast (e.g. too many slots), **3** = requested broadcast of message 8/14 completed, **4** = late reception of a message 7/13 for a valid transaction. |

The data field of ABM/BBM is armoured exactly like a `!AIVDM` payload (chapter 2) and contains the *binary data* of Table 54/57 — the 16-bit DAC/FI and the application data — but **not** the message header: the transceiver adds message ID, repeat indicator, its own MMSI, and (for ABM) sequence number and destination. The sequential message identifier `s` of the ABM becomes the 2-bit sequence number of the message 6 [IEC 61162-1 §8 ABM; M.1371-5 Annex 2 §5.3.1]. The talker of these sentences is whatever the application chooses — the Cornell software used `VT`, the samples in `sample-data/RightwhaleAisProject/20081005-capecod-visitor/20081005-cape-cod-windows/sample message.txt` use `AI` and `EC`.

## 8.3 Decoding one message by hand

### A message 8 as received

Line 8 of [`data/ch08-binary-framing.nmea`](../data/ch08-binary-framing.nmea), recorded at 14:42 UTC on 15 October 2008 by an SR162G next to the Boston (Mystic River) transceiver (`sample-data/RightwhaleAisProject/20081015-boston-pog/log.2008-10-15` line 2447):

```text
!AIVDM,1,1,,A,803OvqQK`PP7fb0Fg7GI63do4T;0,0*51,1224081725.44
```

28 characters, 0 fill bits → 168 bits, one slot. The six-bit groups (Appendix B):

```text
8      0      3      O      v      q      Q      K      `      P      P      7      f      b
001000 000000 000011 011111 111110 111001 100001 011011 101000 100000 100000 000111 101110 101010

0      F      g      7      G      I      6      3      d      o      4      T      ;      0
000000 010110 101111 000111 010111 011001 000110 000011 101100 110111 000100 100100 001011 000000
```

| Bits | Value | Field |
|---|---|---|
| 0–5 | `001000` = 8 | message ID |
| 6–7 | `00` = 0 | repeat indicator |
| 8–37 | `000000001101111111111011100110` = **3 669 734** | source MMSI — a US coast-station identity (00 + MID 366) [M.585-9] |
| 38–39 | `00` = 0 | spare |
| 40–49 | `0101101110` = **366** | DAC: United States |
| 50–55 | `100010` = **34** | FI |
| 56–167 | 112 bits | application data |

That is all message 8 itself can tell us: 112 bits of something that the US authority calls function 34. Chapter 9 §9.3 continues this decode into the right-whale notice (zone 1, "not observed", start day 15 14:42, 10 minutes, a 9 260 m circle at 42.347282° N 70.678317° W). Here the point is the *framing*: 56 header bits, then data, and `data_bits` = 168 − 56 = 112 — a multiple of 8, so no padding question arises.

### A message 6 and its missing acknowledgement

|---|---|---|

| 6–7 | `00` = 0 | repeat indicator |
| 8–37 | `000000001101111111111011010001` = **3 669 713** | source MMSI, a USCG base station |
| 38–39 | `00` = 0 | sequence number |

A base station sent an empty text telegram to an impossible address. The N-AIS feed shows the transaction's whole life: the same payload on channels B and A within a second, then again about 6, 12 and 18 seconds later with the retransmit flag set (`>5Q@` → `>5QB`; `20081013.log` lines 232157–232261), and — because 923156 does not exist — no message 7 anywhere in the 498 505-line file. A second base station, 3 669 971, did exactly the same thing at the same moment (data-file lines 22–23 show its first transmission and its first retry) (§8.6).

### From a `BMS` request to a received `!AIVDM`: the complete send path

The Boston transceiver was driven by a Python script that read buoy status from Cornell's server, encoded a zone notice and handed it to a CNS VDL 6000 Class A transceiver. Its debug log (`sample-data/RightwhaleAisProject/20081015-boston-pog/20081015-pog-update.log` lines 800–815, 15:19 UTC) shows every stage for zone 1:

```text
dac_fi "0101101110100010"
msg    "0000100000000111101111010011000000010110101111000111010111011001000110000011101100110111000100100100001011000000"
bbm_bits 01011011101000100000100000000111101111010011000000010110101111000111010111011001000110000011101100110111000100100100001011000000
pad befaore 128
pad after 132
results Fr881slh5cimnAPs=i92h0 4 4 128 132
bbm_str !VTBBM,1,1,0,0,8,Fr881slh5cimnAPs=i92h0,4*44
sending "!VTBBM,1,1,0,0,8,Fr881slh5cimnAPs=i92h0,4*44"
!AIVDM,2,1,2,A,55MnbnP08S65MU3CCW@Npjr0A8D@LF3GB22222004hA=540Ht6P000000000,0*12
!AIVDM,2,2,2,A,00000000000,2*26
!AIVDO,1,1,,A,803OvqQK`PP7gC0Fg7GI63do4T;0,0*73
$AIABK,,,8,0,3*57
```

1. **The application builds the binary data.** `dac_fi` is 366/34 in binary; `msg` is the 112-bit zone record. Together, 128 bits.
2. **Armouring.** 128 is not a multiple of 6, so the script pads to 132 bits ("pad after 132"), which is 22 characters, and records **4 fill bits**. The first two characters, `F` `r`, are `010110 111010` — the DAC 366 (`0101101110`) and the top four bits of FI 34:
   ```text
   F      r      8      8      1      s      l      h      5      c      i      m      n      A
   010110 111010 001000 001000 000001 111011 110100 110000 000101 101011 110001 110101 110110 010001

   P      s      =      i      9      2      h      0
   100000 111011 001101 110001 001001 000010 110000 000000
   ```
3. **The BBM sentence.** `!VTBBM,1,1,0,0,8,Fr881slh5cimnAPs=i92h0,4*44`: one sentence of one, sequential identifier 0, channel 0 (no preference), message ID 8, the 22 armoured characters, 4 fill bits [IEC 61162-1 §8 BBM].
4. **The transceiver transmits and echoes.** Two position reports from other ships go by, then the transceiver's own `!AIVDO,1,1,,A,803OvqQK`PP7gC0Fg7GI63do4T;0,0*73`: it chose channel A, prepended its header (message 8, repeat 0, MMSI 3 669 734, 2 spare bits) and appended the 128 BBM bits — 168 bits, 28 characters, 0 fill bits. Compare with the `!AIVDM` of the first hand decode: same structure, 37 minutes later (`7fb` vs `7gC` in the start-time field: 14:42 vs 15:19).
5. **The acknowledgement.** `$AIABK,,,8,0,3*57`: no destination (broadcast), no channel given, message ID 8, sequence number 0, type **3** — "requested broadcast of message 8 has been successfully completed" [IEC 61162-1 §8 ABK]. Nine minutes earlier, in the script's first run after start-up (15:10 UTC), the ten zone requests drew `$AIABK,,,8,0,2*56` (type 2, could not be broadcast) seven times (lines 364–485), the other three no ABK at all, and no message 8 appeared among the unit's `!AIVDO` echoes — the only indication the script ever got that nothing went on the air.
6. **Reception elsewhere.** The receiver one desk away logged the same transmissions as `!AIVDM` lines (data-file lines 8–9), and two days earlier the USCG N-AIS network had logged the Provincetown transceiver's equivalent broadcasts from across Massachusetts Bay (lines 14–15).

Upstream of all this sat Cornell's **queue manager**, which handed the radio software lines in a project-internal text format — not a standard, but the kind of glue you will meet in every ASM deployment. From `sample-data/RightwhaleAisProject/20081003-AIS/QM-Cornell-Tampa-2772008.log` line 5 (3 October 2008, 14:10 UTC, Tampa bench test):

```text
BMS,SBNMS,43.000,-68.300,41.000,-71.300,1,1000,2,1223129430,0101101110100010,0000100000000001101110001010000000010110101111000111010111011001000110000011101100110111000100100100001011000000
```

Reading it: a keyword, a site (`SBNMS`, Stellwagen Bank National Marine Sanctuary), a bounding box (43° N 68.3° W to 41° N 71.3° W — presumably the area in which the message should be transmitted), zone 1, a message id (1000 for "not observed", 1001 for "present"), a status (2 or 5), a UNIX time 24 hours ahead of the log time (an expiry), the 16 DAC/FI bits, and the 112 data bits as `0`/`1` text. In the later Provincetown logs the last two fields had already been replaced by the armoured payload and fill count (`…,Fr880a005cimnAPs=i92h0,2*35` in `…/20081005-cape-cod-windows/ARI-Ptown-2792008.log` line 4) — the same kind of payload with **2** fill bits instead of 4, which §8.6 returns to.

## 8.4 The code

Messages 6 and 8 share one idea: read the header, read the DAC/FI, hand everything after it to a registry keyed by `(dac, fi)`. The registry — module `asm` — is new in this chapter; its *contents* (the individual ASM decoders) are the subject of chapter 9. Here we show only its interface and the two message modules.

### Python

[`code/python/aistut/msg_8.py`](../code/python/aistut/msg_8.py):

```python
from . import asm
from .bits import Bits
from .common import require_bits

HEADER_BITS = 56   # message id .. function identifier


def decode(bits: Bits) -> dict:
    """Decode a message 8. Raises DecodeError below 56 bits."""
    require_bits(8, len(bits), HEADER_BITS)
    dac = bits.uint(40, 10)
    fi = bits.uint(50, 6)
    out: dict = {
        "msg_type": 8,
        "repeat_indicator": bits.uint(6, 2),
        "mmsi": bits.uint(8, 30),
        "spare": bits.uint(38, 2),
        "dac": dac,
        "fi": fi,
        "data": bits.raw(HEADER_BITS, len(bits) - HEADER_BITS),
        "data_bits": len(bits) - HEADER_BITS,
    }
    application = asm.decode(dac, fi, bits, HEADER_BITS)
    if application is not None:
        out["application"] = application
    return out
```

```python
    (366, 34): ("USCG/NOAA right whale zone notice (2008)", WHALE_RECORD_BITS, decode_whale_notice),
}


def decode(dac: int, fi: int, bits: Bits, offset: int) -> dict | None:
    """Decode the application data that starts at `offset`.

    Returns None when (dac, fi) is unknown. For a known pair the result
    always carries `application_name`; if the payload is shorter than the
    layout needs it carries an `error` text instead of the fields.
    """
    entry = DECODERS.get((dac, fi))
    if entry is None:
        return None
    name, need, func = entry
    data_bits = len(bits) - offset
    out: dict = {"application_name": name}
    if data_bits < need:
        out["error"] = f"needs {need} data bits, have {data_bits}"
        return out
    out.update(func(bits, offset, data_bits))
    return out
```

Three design decisions, all visible in the output:

* **`data` is always present**, even when `application` is decoded. The raw bits are the ground truth; the decoded fields are an interpretation that may be wrong (§8.2, "a label, not a guarantee").
* **Every ASM decoder takes `(bits, offset, data_bits)`**, never a fixed position, so one function serves message 6 (data at 88), message 8 (data at 56) and, if you extend it, messages 25/26 (data at 40–88, chapter 16).
* **A short payload is not an error of the message.** A message 8 with DAC 1 FI 31 and 100 data bits is still a valid message 8; only the application layer failed. Hence `"error"` inside `application`, not a `DecodeError`.

> **Language notes (Python).** `Callable[[Bits, int, int], dict]` (from `collections.abc`) names a function type, and the `type Decoder = …` statement (PEP 695, Python 3.12+) gives it a short alias; functions are ordinary values, so a dictionary of them is a dispatch table — the same pattern `decode.py` uses for message types. A tuple `(dac, fi)` is hashable and therefore a legal dictionary key. `name, need, func = entry` unpacks a 3-tuple.

The registry in [`code/cpp/asm.hpp`](../code/cpp/asm.hpp):

```cpp
using AsmDecoder = Json (*)(const Bits&, std::size_t, std::size_t);

struct AsmEntry {
    const char* name;
    std::size_t need_bits;
    AsmDecoder func;
};

inline const std::map<std::pair<int, int>, AsmEntry>& registry() {
    static const std::map<std::pair<int, int>, AsmEntry> table = {
        {{1, 0}, {"IFM 0 text telegram", 12, decode_text_telegram}},
        {{1, 11}, {"IMO Circ.236 meteorological and hydrological data", MET_HYDRO_236_BITS, decode_met_hydro_236}},
        {{1, 31}, {"IMO Circ.289 meteorological and hydrographic data", MET_HYDRO_289_BITS, decode_met_hydro_289}},
        {{366, 34}, {"USCG/NOAA right whale zone notice (2008)", WHALE_RECORD_BITS, decode_whale_notice}},
    };
    return table;
}

inline std::optional<Json> decode_asm(int dac, int fi, const Bits& bits, std::size_t offset) {
    const auto& table = asm_detail::registry();
    auto it = table.find({dac, fi});
    if (it == table.end()) return std::nullopt;
    const asm_detail::AsmEntry& entry = it->second;
    std::size_t data_bits = bits.size() - offset;
    Json out = Json::Object{{"application_name", entry.name}};
    if (data_bits < entry.need_bits) {
        out["error"] = "needs " + std::to_string(entry.need_bits) + " data bits, have " +
                       std::to_string(data_bits);
        return out;
    }
    out.update(entry.func(bits, offset, data_bits));
    return out;
}
```

> **Language notes (C++).**
> * `Json (*)(const Bits&, std::size_t, std::size_t)` is a *function pointer type*: "pointer to a function taking these three arguments and returning `Json`". A plain function's name converts to it, so the table can hold `decode_whale_notice` directly.
> * `std::map<std::pair<int,int>, AsmEntry>` works as a dictionary keyed by two integers because `std::pair` already defines `operator<`; `table.find({dac, fi})` builds the pair from a braced list.
> * A `static const` local inside `registry()` is initialised exactly once, on first call — a common idiom for a lookup table that lives next to the code using it.
> * `std::optional<Json>` is the return type for "an object or nothing"; `if (auto app = decode_asm(...))` declares and tests in one go, and `*app` is the contained value.

The registry in [`code/rust/src/asm.rs`](../code/rust/src/asm.rs) is a `match` on a tuple rather than a map:

```rust
pub type AsmDecoder = fn(&Bits, usize, usize) -> Obj;

pub fn lookup(dac: u64, fi: u64) -> Option<(&'static str, usize, AsmDecoder)> {
    match (dac, fi) {
        (1, 0) => Some(("IFM 0 text telegram", 12, decode_text_telegram)),
        (1, 11) => Some(("IMO Circ.236 meteorological and hydrological data", MET_HYDRO_236_BITS, decode_met_hydro_236)),
        (1, 31) => Some(("IMO Circ.289 meteorological and hydrographic data", MET_HYDRO_289_BITS, decode_met_hydro_289)),
        (366, 34) => Some(("USCG/NOAA right whale zone notice (2008)", WHALE_RECORD_BITS, decode_whale_notice)),
        _ => None,
    }
}

pub fn decode(dac: u64, fi: u64, bits: &Bits, offset: usize) -> Option<Json> {
    let (name, need, func) = lookup(dac, fi)?;
    let data_bits = bits.len() - offset;
    let mut out = Obj::new();
    put(&mut out, "application_name", name);
    if data_bits < need {
        put(&mut out, "error", format!("needs {need} data bits, have {data_bits}"));
        return Some(Json::Obj(out));
    }
    out.extend(func(bits, offset, data_bits));
    Some(Json::Obj(out))
}
```

> **Language notes (Rust).**
> * `fn(&Bits, usize, usize) -> Obj` is a function pointer type; every `fn` item with that signature coerces to it, so `match` can return different functions from different arms.
> * `match (dac, fi) { (1, 0) => …, _ => None }` matches on a tuple pattern — no hashing, no map, and the compiler checks that the catch-all `_` arm exists.
> * `lookup(dac, fi)?` inside a function returning `Option` is the `?` operator on an `Option`: it returns `None` early if the lookup failed, exactly as it returns `Err` early on a `Result`.
> * `out.extend(other)` moves every key of another `BTreeMap` into `out` — Python's `dict.update`.
> * `if let Some(app) = …` destructures an `Option` without `unwrap()`.

The registry in [`code/typescript/src/asm.ts`](../code/typescript/src/asm.ts):

```typescript
export type AsmDecoder = (bits: Bits, o: number, dataBits: number) => JsonObject;

interface AsmEntry {
  readonly name: string;
  readonly needBits: number;
  readonly func: AsmDecoder;
}

/** Keyed by "dac/fi" because Map compares array keys by identity, not value. */
export const DECODERS: ReadonlyMap<string, AsmEntry> = new Map<string, AsmEntry>([
  ["1/0", { name: "IFM 0 text telegram", needBits: 12, func: decodeTextTelegram }],
  ["1/11", { name: "IMO Circ.236 meteorological and hydrological data", needBits: MET_HYDRO_236_BITS, func: decodeMetHydro236 }],
  ["1/31", { name: "IMO Circ.289 meteorological and hydrographic data", needBits: MET_HYDRO_289_BITS, func: decodeMetHydro289 }],
  ["366/34", { name: "USCG/NOAA right whale zone notice (2008)", needBits: WHALE_RECORD_BITS, func: decodeWhaleNotice }],
]);

export function decodeAsm(dac: number, fi: number, bits: Bits, offset: number): JsonObject | null {
  const entry = DECODERS.get(`${dac}/${fi}`);
  if (entry === undefined) return null;
  const dataBits = bits.length - offset;
  const out: JsonObject = { application_name: entry.name };
  if (dataBits < entry.needBits) {
    out["error"] = `needs ${entry.needBits} data bits, have ${dataBits}`;
    return out;
  }
  const fields = entry.func(bits, offset, dataBits);
  for (const key of Object.keys(fields)) {
    out[key] = fields[key] as JsonValue;
  }
  return out;
}
```

> **Language notes (TypeScript).**
> * `(bits: Bits, o: number, dataBits: number) => JsonObject` is a function type; `interface AsmEntry` bundles it with a name and a length, and `readonly` keeps the entries immutable.
> * A JavaScript `Map` compares object and array keys by *identity*, so `new Map([[[1, 0], …]])` could never be looked up with a fresh `[1, 0]`. The string key `` `${dac}/${fi}` `` (a template literal) is the usual workaround.
> * `JsonObject | null` with an explicit `!== null` test is how TypeScript expresses an optional result; after the test the compiler *narrows* `app` to `JsonObject`.
> * `Object.keys(fields)` plus a loop is the spread-free way to merge one object into another when the target already has keys.

### Where the four differ

| Concern | Python | C++ | Rust | TypeScript |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Dispatch table | `dict[(int,int), tuple]` | `std::map<std::pair<int,int>, AsmEntry>` | `match (dac, fi)` | `Map<string, AsmEntry>` keyed `"dac/fi"` |
| Function values | `Callable[...]` | function pointer `Json (*)(…)` | `fn(…) -> Obj` | arrow function type |
| "No decoder" result | `None` | `std::nullopt` | `None` | `null` |
| Merging objects | `dict.update` | `Json::update` | `BTreeMap::extend` | `Object.keys` loop |
| Early return on missing | `if entry is None: return None` | `if (it == table.end())` | `lookup(dac, fi)?` | `if (entry === undefined)` |

## 8.6 Pitfalls and real-world deviations

* **Eight extra bits from the fill-bit count.** The Provincetown notices are 176 bits long, the Boston ones 168, for the same 128-bit application payload. The difference is on the sending side: Cornell's radio software declared **2** fill bits for a 22-character BBM (`!VTBBM,1,1,0,0,8,Fr882=7h5cimnAPs=i92h0,2*54`, `sample-data/RightwhaleAisProject/20081003-AIS/AIS_whale` line 1638) where 22 × 6 − 128 = **4** were needed. The transceiver took 130 bits, padded the frame to the next byte boundary [M.1371-5 Annex 2 §3.3.7], and transmitted 40 + 136 = 176 bits; its own echo on the same line, `!AIVDO,1,1,,A,803OvriK`PP8lO0Fg7GI63do4T;000,4*44`, already shows the 30-character payload. CCOM's script declared 4 fill bits and got 168. A receiver sees `data_bits` 120 instead of 112 and must not reject the message for it — but note that a format with a *variable* number of records (chapter 9) now has 8 bits that could be mistaken for the start of another record. Count records only while a whole record fits; report the rest as `trailing_bits`.
* **Retransmit flag and retry timing.** In `20081013.log` the two Boston-area base stations, 3 669 713 and 3 669 971, send the same empty text telegram at 01:42:40 UTC on 14 October 2008 and each repeats it three times, on both channels — four transmissions each, as the rule "the number of retries should be 3" prescribes [Annex 2 §5.3.1]. Both set the retransmit flag from the second transmission on (`>5Q@` → `>5QB`), as they should. What neither does is wait the prescribed 4 s: the repeats come about 6 s apart (receiver `r003669945` timestamps 1223948557, 562, 568, 574 for the first station). A program that de-duplicates addressed messages should key on (source, destination, sequence number) rather than on the flag, which M.1371 only says "should" be set.
* **Nobody checks the destination.** Both stations addressed MMSI 923156, which has six digits and matches no MMSI format [M.585-9]. The transceivers transmitted it four times each and the network relayed it; no layer noticed. A message 7 can never come, so the application-level `ABK` would eventually have reported type 1 ("broadcast but not acknowledged") — if anyone was reading it.
* **The acknowledgement is not delivery.** `$AIABK,,,8,0,3` means the transceiver put the message on the air, nothing more. The Boston log shows seven type-2 ABKs (could not be broadcast) in its first ten-zone run and 26 in all, against 120 type-3: whole rounds of whale notices that never left the building, and the ABK was the only sign.
* **Collisions of meaning.** FI 34 under DAC 366 was the 2008 whale notice; by 2010 the same project registered FI 22 for a redesigned message [AtoN form v4], and Circ.289 uses FI 22 under DAC 1 for its (different) area notice [Circ.289 §11]. A decoder keyed on FI alone will mix all three. Key on `(message type, dac, fi)` and keep the raw bits.
* **DACs that are not MIDs.** Our recordings contain message 8s with DAC 117, 322, 55, 91 and 1003 (the last is in the range Table 23 reserves). One 2007 Norfolk log alone (`sample-data/2007hydro/sr162-noaa-norfolk-telcom2007-05-14`) has 3 517 messages with DAC 55 FI 58 from MMSI 61616. Most are noise or private experiments; none is decodable from public documents. Log the pair, count it, move on.
* **Length is the first validation.** 32-bit "message 8s" exist (data-file line 49) because any 6 bits that happen to read `001000` after a corrupt reception make the receiver label the burst a message 8. Everything below 56 (or 88 for message 6) bits is noise by definition. Between 56 bits and the DAC/FI table's expected length you are in the territory of `"error":"needs N data bits, have M"`.
* **Multi-fragment assembly matters more here than anywhere else.** Messages 6 and 8 (like the safety texts 12 and 14, chapter 12) may run to 1 008 bits, five slots, and so arrive in two or three sentences — three in data-file lines 38–40. Together with messages 5, 19 and 21 they are the messages that routinely need fragment assembly. A lost fragment costs the whole message, and the `Assembler` of chapter 2 must time out stale partial messages or a lost `2,2` will poison the next `2,1` with the same sequential identifier.
